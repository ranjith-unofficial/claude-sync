Read this before choosing any demo

Every famous "watch the AI fail" demo has been fixed. Strawberry letter-counting, basic arithmetic, the classic riddles — they're in training data now, and reasoning models work through them internally.

A failure demo that succeeds is worse than no demo — the room concludes the limitation isn't real and stops trusting you. So this document favours demos that show the mechanism over demos that catch a mistake. Mechanism can't be patched. Where a gamble is worth taking, it's flagged as one, with a fallback.

How to pitch this to an AI&DS class

They're AI majors — they've met neural networks in coursework. So don't teach Part 1 as new information. Teach it as a map they already half-own: "you know what ML is; today we place ChatGPT on that map and then go somewhere your syllabus hasn't."

The survey backs both: 65% asked for "basics of how AI works," and several wrote "overview of AI" / "AI and its subsets" verbatim. The appetite for fundamentals is real — just don't be condescending about it.

10:10 – 10:35Part 1 · 25 min

What is AI, really

Goal: every student can draw the AI → ML → Deep Learning → GenAI → LLM nesting from memory, and say what makes generative different.

1.1  The one-line definition

AI is any system that performs a task we'd normally say requires human intelligence. That's deliberately broad — a chess engine is AI, spam filtering is AI, ChatGPT is AI. The interesting question isn't "is it AI" but "how does it get its rules."

1.2  The shift that made modern AI possible

This is the single most important idea in Part 1, and it's the hinge everything else swings on.

Traditional programmingMachine learning
You give itRules + dataData + answers
It gives youAnswersRules
Spam exampleYou write: if subject contains "lottery" → spamYou show it 100,000 labelled emails; it works out the pattern itself
Say this

"In your first-year programming, you wrote the rules. In machine learning you stop writing rules — you show examples and the machine writes the rules for you. Nobody sat down and programmed ChatGPT with grammar rules. It read, and it worked them out."

1.3  The nesting — draw this on the board

Artificial Intelligence
Any machine doing something "intelligent." 1950s onward.
Machine Learning
Learns rules from data instead of being given them.
Deep Learning
ML using many-layered neural networks. Handles images, audio, text.
Generative AI → LLMs
Creates new content rather than labelling existing content. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini live here.

1.4  Discriminative vs generative — the distinction that matters today

Almost everything in their coursework so far has been discriminative. Today is about the other kind.

DiscriminativeGenerative
Question it answers"Which category is this?""What comes next?"
OutputA label or numberNew content
ExamplesSpam / not-spam · price prediction · face recognitionChatGPT · Midjourney · GitHub Copilot
You've builtThis, in your ML labThis, by 4 p.m. today
Live demo · 3 min

Open any LLM and run the same input twice, back to back:

Classify this review as positive or negative: "The camera is great but the battery dies in 4 hours."
Write a 2-line reply to that reviewer as the brand's support team.

Same model, same box. First is discriminative, second is generative. Point out that an LLM can do both — but the second one is what's new, and it's the reason everyone's talking about this.

Part 1 landing point

"ChatGPT is not a new kind of magic. It's deep learning, pointed at text, trained to generate rather than classify. Everything else today follows from how it generates — which is Part 2."

10:35 – 11:05Part 2 · 30 min

What is an LLM

Goal: every student can explain in their own words why an LLM hallucinates. This is the most-requested topic in the survey — 41% said understanding how AI works is what would make them confident.

2.1  Read the name backwards

  • Model — a mathematical function. Text in, text out.
  • Language — trained on text. Books, code, websites, papers.
  • Large — billions of parameters, trained on trillions of words.

2.2  The entire engine, in one sentence

The core mechanic

An LLM does exactly one thing: given some text, predict the next token. Then it appends that token and does it again. And again. That's the whole engine.

Say this

"It's your phone's autocomplete — except instead of learning from your last 200 messages, it learned from most of the written internet. Everything that feels intelligent about it is a side effect of doing that one trick extremely well, billions of times."

2.3  Tokens are not words

A token is roughly 4 characters — sometimes a whole word, sometimes a fragment. "unbelievable" might split into un | believ | able. The model never sees letters. It sees token IDs.

Do not use the "count the r's in strawberry" demo

It became a famous benchmark, so it's in training data, and reasoning models now spell the word out internally before answering. It gets it right. Show the mechanism instead — that can't be patched.

Live demo A · show the tokenizer itself · cannot fail

Open a tokenizer visualiser (platform.openai.com/tokenizer) and type words in live. It colours each token and shows the IDs.

Good words to try — let them predict the split before you press enter:

strawberry unbelievable antidisestablishmentarianism Thiruvananthapuram def calculate_gradient(x):

They'll see common words survive whole, rare words shatter into fragments, and code split in ways that look arbitrary. The point lands without needing the model to fail: this is what it reads. Not letters. Not words. These chunks.

Live demo B · Tamil vs English · cannot fail, and it's local

Put the same sentence in both languages into the tokenizer and compare token counts.

Tamil costs substantially more tokens than English for identical meaning. Real, visible, checkable — with consequences they care about: Indian-language AI is more expensive to run, slower, and fills the context window faster. Nothing to do with the language being "harder" — the tokenizer was mostly trained on English. One of the best 90 seconds in the session for this room.

Optional gamble — test it Thursday before you rely on it

If you want a live failure, character-count generation is still structurally hard, because the model has no counter for its own output: Write a sentence about databases that is exactly 62 characters long, including spaces. Count it on screen. Test this on the exact model you'll demo on — and have Demo A ready as the fallback if it succeeds.

2.4  Weights: what it kept, and what it lost

Training compresses trillions of words into billions of numbers. Those numbers — the weights — are frozen when training ends. The training text is not stored anywhere.

Analogy

"You've read 200 textbooks. Ask you about photosynthesis and you'll answer well. Ask you to quote page 143 of book number 7 and you can't — but under viva pressure you might produce something that sounds like a quote. The model is permanently in that viva."

2.5  The context window is its entire world

The context window is everything the model can see right now — your prompt, the conversation so far, any file you attached. Outside that window, nothing exists.

  • It has no memory between chats. A new chat is a stranger. It doesn't "remember you from yesterday."
  • Attaching a file just pastes text into the window. There's no filing cabinet.
  • Very long inputs degrade. Material buried in the middle gets recalled worst.
Live demo · 2 min · cannot fail

Open a brand-new chat and ask: What did I ask you about earlier?

It has no idea. Obvious once seen — and it kills the "the AI knows me" intuition that quietly wrecks their prompts.

2.6  Temperature

At each step the model has a ranked list of likely next tokens. Temperature decides how boldly it picks.

  • Low (0–0.3) — nearly always takes the top choice. Repeatable. Use for facts, extraction, code.
  • High (0.8–1.0) — samples further down. Varied. Use for brainstorming, creative writing.

Same prompt three times at high temperature gives three different answers. That's not a bug — it's sampling. Note: most chat UIs don't expose temperature; you'll see it in the API, and in n8n this afternoon.

2.7  Why it hallucinates — the payoff

Now assemble the pieces they already have. Let them get there before you say it.

  1. It generates statistically plausible continuations.
  2. It has weights, not a database — nothing is ever looked up.
  3. It has no mechanism to check truth, and no signal for "I don't know."
  4. So when it doesn't know, it produces the most plausible-looking text instead. Confidently.
The sentence to put on a slide

Hallucination is not a bug in the system. It is the system. The model is always generating plausible text — you only call it hallucination when plausible and true happen to diverge.

Live demo · the hook from 10:00, now explained
Give me 3 research papers on [a narrow topic from their current unit], with authors, journal and year.

Titles that sound perfect. Plausible authors. Real-looking journals. Check one live — it likely won't exist. Then say: "At 10 a.m. you saw this happen. Now you can explain it. And in twenty minutes you'll be able to prevent it."

This demo has the same fragility — protect it
  • Turn web search / browsing OFF. With search on, the model looks it up and answers correctly — and your hook dies exactly the way strawberry did.
  • Pick a genuinely narrow topic from their syllabus, not a famous one. Broad topics have real papers it can recall.
  • Test three candidate topics on Thursday and keep the one that fabricates most confidently. Screenshot it as a backup in case the live run behaves.
Part 2 landing point

"It predicts, it doesn't look up. Remember that one line and everything for the rest of the day is predictable — including why our agent this afternoon needs tools."

11:15 – 11:35Part 3 · 20 min  ·  after the 10-min break

How to actually use an LLM

Goal: they stop treating it as one box called "ChatGPT" and start choosing an interface and a mode on purpose.

3.1  Four ways to reach the same model

InterfaceWhat it isUse it when
Chat UIchatgpt.com, claude.ai, gemini.google.comThinking, drafting, learning. Where all of you live now.
Projects / Gems / Custom instructionsA saved chat workspace with persistent context and filesYou keep re-explaining the same background. Set it once.
APIYour code calls the model directlyYou need it inside a program, at scale, or automated.
Embedded in a productCopilot in your editor, AI in SheetsThe work already lives in that tool.

Point out the gap in the room: 94% of them use the chat UI. Under a quarter have touched anything else. This afternoon's agent runs on the API — through a visual tool, so no code required.

3.2  What it's genuinely good and bad at

StrongWeak
Rewriting, summarising, reformattingPrecise counting and self-measurement
Explaining a concept five different waysAnything after its training cutoff
Writing and debugging codeFacts about you, your college, your syllabus
Structure: turning mess into a tableCitations and references (see Part 2)
First drafts of anythingSaying "I don't know"

The pattern: strong when the answer is derivable from what's in front of it; weak when it needs a fact it must retrieve. Every weakness in that right column is fixed the same way — put the information into the context window, or give it a tool. That's the entire afternoon.

3.3  The habit to install today

  1. Give it the source. Don't ask about your unit — paste your unit in.
  2. Ask for the reasoning, not just the answer. You can't check an answer you can't follow.
  3. Verify anything you'd be embarrassed to be wrong about. Names, numbers, citations, dates.
  4. Iterate. The first output is a draft. Reply "too generic, use my example" instead of starting over.

That fourth point speaks straight to the 37% who said "I don't get the answer I expected" — most of them retry from scratch instead of correcting in place.

11:35 – 12:00Part 4 · 25 min  ·  includes hands-on

Prompt engineering

Goal: they can take a bad prompt and fix it deliberately using a checklist — not by retrying until something works. Targets the 44% who lose the most time writing prompts and deciding what to ask.

4.1  What it actually is

Prompt engineering is the practice of structuring your input so the model's next-token prediction lands where you want it. It is not magic words. It follows entirely from Part 2 — which is why we did Part 2 first.

4.2  The anatomy — six parts

Not every prompt needs all six. Most bad prompts are missing four.

PartQuestion it answersExample
RoleWho should it be?"You are an ML tutor for 2nd-year AI & DS students."
ContextWhat must it know?"We've covered linear regression but not neural networks."
TaskWhat exactly to do?"Explain batch, stochastic and mini-batch gradient descent."
FormatWhat shape is the output?"One table per variant: how it works, when to use, trade-off."
ConstraintsWhat are the limits?"Under 300 words. No calculus notation."
ExamplesCan you show it?One sample of the output you want.

4.3  Before and after

✕ What they write now
explain gradient descent

Result: a generic textbook dump. Wrong depth, wrong length, unknown audience, and probably drowns them in calculus they haven't done yet.

✓ What to write instead
You are an ML tutor for 2nd-year
AI & Data Science students.

We have covered linear regression
and cost functions, but NOT neural
networks yet.

Explain batch, stochastic and
mini-batch gradient descent using
ONE running example: fitting a line
to 1000 student marks.

Format: one table per variant with
columns — How it works | When to
use it | The trade-off.

Under 300 words. No calculus
notation. Then tell me in one line
which one I'd use for my final year
project and why.

Run both live. The difference is not subtle, and it does more to convince the room than any slide.

4.4  Four techniques worth naming

TechniqueWhat you doBest for
Zero-shotJust describe the taskSimple, common tasks
Few-shotGive 2–3 examples of input→output firstSpecific formats, consistent tone
Chain of thoughtAdd "work through this step by step"Logic, maths, multi-step reasoning
GroundingPaste the source and say "answer only from this"Anything factual. The hallucination fix.
Live demo · grounding — the bridge to this afternoon

Ask the same syllabus question twice:

1. What are the assumptions of linear regression?
2. [attach their unit PDF] Using only the attached notes, list the assumptions of linear regression exactly as our syllabus defines them, and quote the line each one comes from.

The second is checkable, matches what the examiner wants, and can't invent citations. Say: "You didn't get a better model. You gave it better input. That's grounding — and it's the first thing we'll build into our agent."

4.5  Hands-on — 12 minutes

In pairs. Give every pair the same weak prompt tied to a unit they're doing now:

Exercise prompt
explain overfitting

Task: rewrite it using the six-part anatomy. Run both. Then: three pairs read their before and after aloud. Ask the room which added part made the biggest difference — the answer is almost always context or format, and that's the lesson.

Close the morning here

"Everything you've done today, the model has only talked. It answered questions. It never looked anything up, never saved anything, never did anything. After lunch we give it hands — and you'll build that yourself, from an empty screen."

14:00 – 14:30Part 5 · 30 min

What is an AI agent

Goal: they can name the four components and explain what separates an agent from a chatbot — before they build one.

5.1  Open by showing the finished thing · 6 min

Run your working agent on the projector with the Google Sheet open beside it. Type one instruction:

The money shot
Prepare me for Unit 3.

Then say nothing. The room watches it work through the notes, look something up, and write six rows into the sheet, one after another — from a single instruction, with nobody typing. "That's an agent. You gave it a goal, not a question, and it did the work."

5.2  What is actually an agent — the honest ladder

Most things marketed as "AI agents" are not agents. Give them the ladder so they can tell the difference for the rest of their careers.

LevelYou give itIt doesAgent?
Chatbota questionanswersNo
+ Retrieval (RAG)a questionlooks up → answersNo
+ Toolsa questionpicks a tool → answersStill no
Agenta goalplans → acts → observes → re-plans → deliversYes
Multi-agenta goaldelegates to other agentsYes
The test — give them this sentence

Does it do multi-step work toward a goal while you are not typing? If you have to prompt every step, it's a chatbot with tools. Adding tools to a chatbot does not make it an agent.

Say this

"This morning you had a consultant — it told you things. An agent is the assistant who picks up the phone and actually does it. And the difference isn't intelligence. It's that you handed over a goal instead of a question."

5.3  How an agent thinks — the loop

Draw this on the board. It's the one genuinely new idea of the afternoon, and everything they build maps onto it.

The loop
GOAL received ↓ THINK what's my first step? ACT call a tool OBSERVE read the result THINK does that change my plan? am I done? ← the agent ↺ loop until the goal is met ↓ DELIVER a result, or an action taken

That second THINK is the whole thing. The result of one action changes what the next action is. A chatbot has no such step — it answers and stops. This pattern has a name in the literature: ReAct (Reason + Act).

Traced on our actual agent

Student types: "Prepare me for Unit 3."
THINK → "A goal, not a question. First I need to know what's in Unit 3." ACT → reads the notes in context OBSERVE → 6 topics found THINK → "Topic 4 is barely covered. That's a gap." ACT → calls Wikipedia("regularization") OBSERVE → solid explanation returned THINK → "Now I can write a question for every topic." ACT → writes row 1 to the sheet ACT → writes row 2 to the sheet ... (repeats for all 6) THINK → "Goal met. Report back." DELIVER → "Logged 6 topics. Topic 4 was thin in your notes so I filled it from Wikipedia."

Nobody programmed that sequence. No if statement decided topic 4 was thin. No loop counter said "six rows." The model decided how many steps to take and when it was finished.

5.4  The four components

ComponentWhat it isIn our build
Model — the brainThe predictor from this morningGemini
Tools — the handsFunctions it can call to affect the worldWikipedia · Calculator
Loop — the judgementDecide → act → observe → decide againn8n's AI Agent node
Memory — the continuityState that outlives one messageSimple Memory

5.4  The loop, concretely

Walk this through on the board slowly. It's the one genuinely new idea of the afternoon.

Student asks: "What's the latest on the DPDP Act? Is it in my notes?"
REASON → "My notes were provided. Let me check them first." ACT → calls search_notes("DPDP Act") OBSERVE → nothing relevant found REASON → "Not in the notes. I have a web tool." ACT → calls search_web("DPDP Act latest 2026") OBSERVE → three current articles returned REASON → "I can answer now, and I must flag that this was not from the syllabus." ANSWER → replies, labelled clearly

Nobody programmed that sequence. No if statement chose the web search. The model decided, mid-task, which tool to reach for. That decision is the whole difference — and it's why this is an agent and not a flowchart.

Say this plainly

"An agent is not a smarter model. It's the same model from this morning, given tools and permission to choose. The intelligence you'll see this afternoon is mostly in the wiring — which is good news, because the wiring is what you're about to learn."

5.5  Accounts and keys · 12 min

Design decision — one credential, total

Setup friction is the biggest threat to the build. Every extra signup loses students permanently. So the stack was cut to exactly one API key:

ComponentWhat it costs the student
n8n accountSign in with Google · ~30 sec
Gemini API keyGoogle AI Studio · free, instant, no card
Wikipedia toolNo credential at all
Calculator toolNo credential at all
Simple MemoryNo credential at all

Rejected: SerpAPI/Tavily web search (extra signup + key) and Google Sheets logging (OAuth flow that breaks in workshops). Wikipedia proves the identical concept — the model chooses which tool to call — at zero setup cost. Both rejected pieces are listed as stretch goals below for fast finishers.

Do not share one API key across the room

Tempting shortcut, guaranteed failure. Gemini free-tier rate limits are per key and low — 54 students hitting one key at once throttles everybody within seconds, and you'll spend the build debugging quota errors instead of teaching. Each student needs their own key. This is why Wednesday's pre-work is non-negotiable rather than a convenience.

Run this while the concept settles. Walk the room — this is where people get stuck, not later.

  1. Everyone opens n8n and signs in with Google
  2. Google AI Studio → Create API key → copy it somewhere safe
  3. Have their unit notes open and ready to copy
  4. Hands up if anything failed — pair those students with a neighbour now, not at 15:00

For students who arrive with nothing: getting a Gemini key takes about a minute, so recover them here rather than writing them off — but pair them with someone who's ready so they're never watching a blank screen.

14:30 – 15:45Part 6 · 75 min  ·  built from scratch

The build — from an empty canvas

Goal: every student has a running "Study Buddy" agent they assembled node by node, and can explain what each node does. No template, no import.

Ground rules
  • Nothing is pre-built. They start from File → New Workflow, empty.
  • Nobody moves on until their step runs. Checkpoints below; ask for hands each time.
  • Scope is deliberately small. Two tools, not four. A finished small agent beats an unfinished ambitious one — and it's what they'll present at 15:45.
  • You build on the projector at the same pace. They copy what they see.

What they're building

Unit Prep Agent — give it a goal, it does the work. From the single instruction Prepare me for Unit 3 it:

  1. Works through their notes and identifies the key topics
  2. Judges which topics its notes cover thinly ← a real decision, not a script
  3. Looks those up on Wikipedia
  4. Writes one row per topic into their revision sheet, with a practice question
  5. Reports back what it did

Then a schedule trigger makes it run at 8 p.m. without them there.

Why this one passes the agent test
  • Goal in, not a question in — one instruction, many actions
  • Multi-step and self-directed — nobody told it there'd be six topics, or which were thin
  • Each step depends on the last — it can't know what to look up until it's read the notes
  • It acts on the world — rows in a real sheet, not text in a chat
  • It runs without them — the 8 p.m. trigger is the clincher

Setup cost: one API key + one Google connection. Wikipedia and the scheduler need no credentials.

Turn it from a chatbot into an agent

In the AI Agent node, open System Message. This single field is what decides whether they've built a chatbot or an agent. Type it out together:

You are an exam prep agent for a 2nd-year AI & Data Science student. When given a goal like "prepare me for Unit 3": 1. Work through the notes and list the key topics. 2. For each topic, judge whether the notes cover it well enough to revise from. 3. Look up anything thin using your tools. 4. Checkpoint: ask "who are you?" — the answer should have changed. Teaching beat: point at the last two lines. "Work through the entire goal" and "don't ask permission between steps" are what make it act instead of chat. Delete those two lines and you have a chatbot again.

Try to break it

Ask it to prepare a unit whose notes it doesn't have. Ask in Tamil. Give it a goal with no clear endpoint. Ask about something too recent for Wikipedia — watching it fall back to guessing is the lesson.

This is the responsible-AI content, delivered as engineering rather than a lecture — and it closes the loop with the 20% who didn't know AI had limits at all.

Stretch goals — for students who finish early only
  • Calculator tool — no credentials; lets it handle the stats questions in their own coursework.
  • Real web search — SerpAPI or Tavily free tier, for anything more current than Wikipedia.
  • Email or Telegram delivery — the 8 p.m. run messages them instead of only writing to the sheet.
If Google OAuth defeats a student

Don't let them sit dead for 20 minutes. Have them skip to Step 6 and give the agent a goal that ends in a written summary rather than sheet rows. They still have a goal-directed, multi-step, autonomously-scheduled agent — they've just lost the visual. Better than losing the student.

Prep checklist

Send to students Wednesday night

Deliberately only two things to sign up for. Keep it that short — every extra item loses people.

  1. Create a free n8n account (Sign in with Google is fastest)
  2. Go to Google AI Studio → Create API key → paste it somewhere you can find on Friday. Free, instant, no card needed. Get your own — don't share with a friend, or you'll both hit rate limits mid-session.
  3. Make sure you can sign in to Google Sheets on your laptop — the agent will write to a sheet you own
  4. Bring 2–3 pages of one unit's notes as text you can copy — the agent runs on this. Without it you'll be building on someone else's material.
